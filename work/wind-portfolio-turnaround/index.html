Problem

A loss-making unit, staffed at twice its contractual need, supporting hardware that never stops — and knowledge locked in a few senior engineers.

Approach

Standardised the work and moved it to the right level first, split one blended SLA into three, started charging for out-of-scope work, then resized the team and staged AI in.

Outcome

Profit of ~15% in six months, ~25% later. 99.9% SLA held, incident fixes 8× faster, 70–80% of the portfolio automated, and several team awards.

Context

The situation

The unit supported about 55 applications that monitor wind turbines for a global OEM — mission-critical systems with globally distributed hardware that need 24×7 uptime. The contract was worth more than $2M a year.

When I took over, the unit was running at a complete loss. It had roughly twice the people the contract needed, critical knowledge sat with a few senior engineers, support, enhancements and changes were measured as one blended pool, and extra work was being delivered for free.

Constraints

What couldn’t move

24×7 systems, no maintenance windowsStaffed at ~2× contractual needKnowledge held by a few peopleOne blended SLA hiding failuresOut-of-scope work delivered freeSenior client IT relationship under strain
Key decisions

The calls I made, and what each one cost

Fix the operating model before reducing people

Standard operating procedures replaced knowledge held in individuals, repetitive work moved to a new L1.5 support layer, and only then was the team resized.

Trade-off acceptedIt delayed the visible saving by months — but the reduction didn’t push risk onto the people who stayed.

Measure support, enhancements and changes separately

Each stream got its own target instead of one blended number.

Trade-off acceptedThree targets are harder to report than one, but they showed exactly where the service was failing.

Charge for out-of-scope work

Requests outside the contract became separately scoped, paid work — about $200K a year of new business.

Trade-off acceptedSome uncomfortable conversations with the client, handled through a fixed reporting and escalation routine.

Bring AI in by stages

AI first watched support tickets in shadow mode, then handled a six-hour window, then full days. Each stage needed evidence from the one before.

Trade-off acceptedSlower than a big-bang switch-over. It reached production and was never rolled back.

My role

Who did what

What I owned

The P&L, the operating-model redesign, the split of service commitments, the staged introduction of AI, incident leadership and the client relationship.

What the team delivered

Day-to-day support, enhancements and automation across the portfolio, by a 20-person core team and a 15-person L1.5 team.

What the client decided

Service levels, contractual scope, and acceptance of the commercial changes.

Results

What changed

  • From a complete loss to ~15% profit within six months, then ~25%.
  • 99.9% SLA held across ~55 applications that run around the clock.
  • Time to fix a production issue cut from 4 days to under 12 hours; automation covers 70–80% of the portfolio.
  • About $200K a year of previously free work turned into billable scope; the team won several internal awards.
Where AI fits

AI with a named owner

AI for ticket routing and closure reached production through a staged rollout — shadow mode, a six-hour window, then full days — and was never rolled back.

Takeaway

What I carry forward

Reduce people last. Standardise the knowledge, move work to the right level, then resize. And a single blended number is where failure hides — separate targets cost simplicity and reveal the truth.
