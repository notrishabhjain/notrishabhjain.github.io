01 · Situation

A statutory commission still running on a spreadsheet

The 8th Central Pay Commission receives memoranda and demands from staff associations, ministries and federations, examines them, and places its recommendations. When I picked up the engagement, that process ran on an Excel workbook, and the Commission works against a tenure fixed in statute.

The platform had to give officers a governed workflow, take in the Commission's existing Excel data, migrated from 14 offices that shared no common data structure, and go live while coordinating four separate bodies.

02 · The constraint

The date could not move, so something else had to give

On most programmes, when requirements churn or a dependency slips, the date moves. Here it could not. So every other variable had to take the strain: the shape of the scope, the sequencing, the team structure and how coordination was run.

  • A statutory tenure fixing the delivery date
  • An Excel workbook to replace with an accountable workflow
  • Volumes expected to rise by an order of magnitude within months of launch
  • Four coordinating bodies with no reporting line between them
  • Government of India requirements for open-source technology and government-cloud hosting
03 · My mandate

Deliver it, define it, then add the AI layer

I joined on 23 July 2026 as Project Manager, leading a team of eight. I owned delivery, product and workflow definition, and stakeholder coordination. After launch, I led the move from the first operational platform towards an AI-assisted examination layer, and I wrote its scope.

06 · Workflow and rules

Every demand moves through clear stages

A memorandum is a submission. A demand is a single point the Commission has to decide on, and it is the only unit anything is measured in. Five roles use the platform: Processing Officer, Reviewing Officer, Concerned Ministry Officer, a Dashboard and Reports user, and Admin.

The user-facing modules are Memoranda Home, Demand Creation, Examination, Referred by Other Officer, Review, Demand Management, Dashboard, and Reports and Analytics. The as-built specification groups the whole system into 11 modules.

07 · Decisions

Three calls, each with its cost

Decision 1. Parallelise the delivery rather than sequence it.

WhySequencing the modules would have put every slip straight onto the statutory date. Running them as parallel streams across two sprints moved schedule risk into coordination, which I could manage, and away from the deadline, which I could not. The original plan had five sprints, and I cut it to two.

Trade-off acceptedParallel streams cost more coordination and more of my own time in the middle of the programme, and with a team of eight that was real. It bought a delivery shape where no single module could sink the date.

Decision 2. Turn the spreadsheet into a proper workflow, not a nicer spreadsheet.

WhyA shared file has no state, no owner and no audit trail, so work stalls invisibly. I specified the demand as a state machine, from creation through examination, ministry reply, peer referral and reviewing officer to placement before the Commission, with role-based access for each officer.

Trade-off acceptedIt is slower than a spreadsheet for a single quick edit, and it asked officers to learn a process rather than a file. That is the price of being able to say who holds a demand and what happened to it.

Decision 3. Build for month six, not launch day.

WhyThe known trajectory was tens of thousands of demands within months, and about 200,000 within six. Designing for launch-day volume would have meant rebuilding inside the same statutory window.

Trade-off acceptedDesigning for roughly ten times the live volume spent effort on load the platform did not yet carry, which is hard to justify against a four-week clock. It avoided a re-architecture during the Commission's tenure, when there would have been no room for one.

08 · Architecture and data

A plain system, loaded from a workbook

The front end is React, the API is Node.js with Express, and the database is PostgreSQL. Officers sign in through Parichay SSO, which is live. Sessions use signed tokens bound to the device, and access is by role. The schema has 29 tables and a view. The action history and audit log cannot be changed after the fact, because the database revokes the permission and a trigger blocks it. The platform is hosted in a government cloud behind a firewall.

Loading the data

  1. Excel dumpmemoranda and demands
  2. Import scriptsfive, run by an engineer
  3. Exact-duplicate hashidentical items flagged
  4. Contacts encryptedemail and mobile
  5. PostgreSQL~21,000 demands

There was no legacy system to migrate from, so this was a one-time load of the Commission's existing Excel data, which came in from 14 offices with no common structure. Gaps in the data were surfaced as "Not Specified" instead of being filled in or hidden.

09 · Delivery

Four bodies, no reporting line, one date

Across four bodies that do not report to each other, the only levers are institutional, not managerial. Coordination ran through the proper channels rather than through requests, and the Excel data load ran as its own stream so it could not block module delivery.

Reporting ran on a fixed pattern: a weekly steering committee, and status reports to the Commission's director listing every dependency with its owner, current status and next step. I presented a progress review at Additional Secretary level. An external security audit ran from 19 to 21 August, and the platform has 748 automated checks on its dashboard.

How the delivery used AI

The programme ran as an AI-assisted lifecycle: AI support in requirements, review, delivery, discovery and documentation, with a person accountable for every output. The lifecycle is set out on the Method page.

The platform is designed for much larger volumes than it holds today, while staying aligned to Government of India requirements for open-source technology and government-cloud hosting. Demand creation went from about 50 a day to about 1,000.

The AI layer, and what it is not allowed to do

Scoped, not builtNot part of the live platform

I wrote the scope for the AI examination layer: duplicate detection, so that the same demand arriving through several associations is recognised as one; anomaly detection; evidence matching against the memoranda a demand cites; and retrieval of the referenced sources. The objective is that officers stop reading every page to find the one that matters. It is not to automate the examination.

Duplicates are graded by similarity: identical at 0.99 and above, near between 0.60 and 0.99, and related between 0.40 and 0.60. The use cases grew from seven to nine to eleven as the work was scoped. The layer is advisory only and separate from Phase 1, and the delivery is planned as a two-month split.

The division of labour is deliberate. Power BI gives analytical visualisation and the AI gives examination support. Neither is asked to do the other's job, and neither makes the decision.

12 · What I carry forward

Decide early what gives when the date will not

Where a date is genuinely immovable, the useful question is not how to go faster but which variable is allowed to absorb the risk. Naming that early, here coordination overhead, is what makes the rest of the plan honest.

Escalation and diagnosis are parallel tracks, not a sequence. Raising an issue through the right channel does not mean waiting for it.

Read what is already built before proposing what is new. Several of the places AI was wrong here were places where the as-built specification already had the answer.
