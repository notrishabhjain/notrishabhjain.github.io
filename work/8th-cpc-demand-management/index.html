Case study · 8th Central Pay Commission · Government of India

Eight modules in about four weeks, against a date set by statute

A national memorandum-processing platform built across four bodies with no reporting line between them. A spreadsheet became a governed demand lifecycle, and the AI examination layer is now being added on top.

01 · Situation

A statutory commission still running on a spreadsheet

The 8th Central Pay Commission receives memoranda and demands from staff associations, ministries and federations, examines them, and places its recommendations. When I picked up the engagement, that process ran on a spreadsheet, and the Commission works against a tenure fixed in statute.

The platform had to serve 10 processing officers and roughly 100 ministry users, absorb a migration from 14 offices that shared no data structure, and go live while coordinating four separate bodies.

02 · The constraint

The date was the one variable that could not absorb risk

On most programmes, when requirements churn or a dependency slips, the date moves. Here it could not. So every other variable had to take the strain: the shape of the scope, the sequencing, the team structure and how coordination was run.

  • A statutory tenure fixing the delivery date
  • A shared spreadsheet to replace with an accountable workflow
  • Volumes rising by an order of magnitude within months of launch
  • Four coordinating bodies with no reporting line between them
  • Migration from 14 offices with no shared structure
  • Government of India requirements for open-source technology and government-cloud hosting
03 · My mandate

Deliver it, define it, then add the AI layer

I led project delivery, product and workflow definition, and stakeholder coordination. After launch, I led the move from the first operational platform towards an AI-assisted examination layer, and I wrote its scope.

04 · Decisions

Three calls, each with its cost

Decision 1. Parallelise the delivery rather than sequence it.

WhySequencing eight modules would have put every slip straight onto the statutory date. Running them as parallel streams across two sprints moved schedule risk into coordination, which I could manage, and away from the deadline, which I could not.

Trade-off acceptedParallel streams cost more coordination and more of my own time in the middle of the programme, and with seven people that was real. It bought a delivery shape where no single module could sink the date.

Decision 2. Turn the spreadsheet into a governed workflow, not a nicer spreadsheet.

WhyA shared file has no state, no owner and no audit trail, so work stalls invisibly. I specified the demand as a state machine, from creation through examination, ministry reply, peer referral and reviewing officer to placement before the Commission, with role-based access for the processing officers and the ministry users.

Trade-off acceptedIt is slower than a spreadsheet for a single quick edit, and it asked officers to learn a process rather than a file. That is the price of being able to say who holds a demand and what happened to it.

Decision 3. Build for month six, not launch day.

WhyThe system went live with roughly 1,500 memoranda and 50,000 demands. The known trajectory was 18,000 memoranda and some 200,000 demands as the next tranche of data arrived. Designing for launch-day volume would have meant rebuilding inside the same statutory window.

Trade-off acceptedDesigning for ten times the live volume spent effort on load the platform did not yet carry, which is hard to justify against a four-week clock. It avoided a re-architecture during the Commission's tenure, when there would have been no room for one.

05 · Delivery

Four bodies, no reporting line, one date

Across four bodies that do not report to each other, the only levers are institutional, not managerial. Coordination ran through the proper channels rather than through requests, and the 14-office migration ran as its own stream so it could not block module delivery.

When vendor resolution stalled, I escalated through the institutional channel and had the underlying SSL and routing fault diagnosed from the technology side at the same time. Against a statutory date, waiting on one track while the other was available was not a defensible choice.

The platform is designed for much larger volumes than it launched with, while staying aligned to Government of India requirements for open-source technology and government-cloud hosting. Demand creation went from about 50 a day to about 1,000.

The AI layer, and what it is not allowed to do

I wrote the scope for the AI examination layer: duplicate detection, so that the same demand arriving through several associations is recognised as one; anomaly detection; evidence matching against the memoranda a demand cites; and retrieval of the referenced sources. The objective is that officers stop reading every page to find the one that matters. It is not to automate the examination.

The division of labour is deliberate. Power BI gives analytical visualisation and the AI gives examination support. Neither is asked to do the other's job, and neither makes the decision.

07 · What I carry forward

Name the variable that absorbs the risk

Where a date is genuinely immovable, the useful question is not how to go faster but which variable is allowed to absorb the risk. Naming that early, here coordination overhead, is what makes the rest of the plan honest.

Escalation and diagnosis are parallel tracks, not a sequence. Raising an issue through the right channel does not mean waiting for it.
