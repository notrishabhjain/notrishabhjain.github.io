Case study · MNRE · National Green Hydrogen Mission

A national registry for a process that did not yet exist

India had no digital certification process for green hydrogen, so the process had to be defined before anything could be built. The registry is append-only, passed an external security audit, and has an AI layer scoped to stay advisory.

01 · Situation

There was no as-is process to digitise

The Green Hydrogen Certification of India scheme sits under the National Green Hydrogen Mission at MNRE. India had no established digital certification process for green hydrogen of this kind, so there was no mature process to take and automate.

Process definition, product design, delivery, stakeholder coordination, security certification and the later AI design all had to happen together rather than in sequence.

02 · The constraint

Define it, build it and pass the audit, all at once

  • No existing certification process, and so no benchmark to build against
  • Four certificate types, each needing an auditable record of every amendment
  • An external security audit by a CERT-In empanelled auditor standing between the build and production
  • Validation rules that change through office memoranda, not through releases
  • Auditors who had never operated the digital process they were about to be measured on
03 · My mandate

From process definition to a Phase 2 AI scope

As Project Manager I led delivery from process definition through to production, turned the emerging certification process into a production-ready national registry, and wrote the scope for the Phase 2 AI layer.

04 · How it was run

Defining a process that did not exist

The scheme guidelines were the starting document. Each step below fed the next, and nothing went to development until the process flow had been presented to and accepted by the client.

Requirements

The BRD was prepared by the business analysts, and I reviewed and presented it in the regular client connects. The client Director signed it. Nothing in it changed after sign-off. Later changes went in as change requests, recorded in an annexure to the BRD.

Specification

An SRS of close to 250 requirements, traced through a requirements traceability matrix from requirement to function to feature. That produced the feature list, and the bug list was built from it.

Team and cadence

A team of eight, built in-house with no external vendor: two business analysts, three full-stack developers, a tech lead, QA, UI/UX and DevOps. About nine two-week sprints, tracked in OpenForge, an internal NIC tool, with a demo every three sprints.

AI in the delivery

Claude supported requirement and impact analysis, documents, test cases and code, with a human reviewing every output. The team logged the gains itself. See the Method page.

05 · Decisions

Three calls, each with its cost

Decision 1. Design the registry append-only.

WhyA certificate is a legal artefact. If an amendment overwrites the original, the evidence of how the decision was reached is destroyed. So an amendment becomes a new record while the original stays intact, across all four certificate types. Concept and Facility certificates, for example, can be amended for an expansion, an ownership transfer or a new unit.

Trade-off acceptedAppend-only costs storage, makes every read path more complex, and means the interface has to explain which version a user is looking at. It buys a trail that survives a challenge to a certification years later.

Decision 2. Run an internal security review before the external audit.

WhyIf the external audit is the moment issues surface, the production date sits at the mercy of a report. The programme reviewed the code internally and fixed things in parallel, so the external audit met a platform that had already been attacked.

Trade-off acceptedIt pulled security effort forward into a window already competing with feature delivery, and some of that effort would have been unnecessary had the audit been lenient. It produced a short fix-and-revalidate round instead of a long remediation cycle.

Decision 3. Keep AI out of Phase 1, deliberately.

WhyAuditors needed to understand and operate the underlying process before they could judge an assistant working on top of it. An AI suggestion that nobody can evaluate is one nobody should trust.

Trade-off acceptedIt delayed the efficiency gain by a full phase and meant launching with manual effort we knew could be reduced. It meant Phase 2 could be accepted on evidence rather than on faith.

06 · Delivery

Security as a delivery stream, not a gate at the end

48 of 51 findings from the internal security review were closed before the external audit opened. The three that remained came from customised reusable components. They were verified by hand and the residual risk was signed by the CTO rather than carried quietly, which is the difference between an accepted risk and an unowned one.

The external audit then needed one short fix-and-revalidate round, and the certificate was issued on 30 June 2026 with no Critical or High findings.

The AI layer, and the two guardrails on it

I wrote the scope for Phase 2. It covers document intelligence on submissions, emissions anomaly detection, a verification assistant for auditors, application summaries for certification bodies, compliance pattern intelligence, and process-risk prediction.

AI advises. A named officer approves every consequential action.

Every generated claim cites the document supporting it.

Neither guardrail is negotiable, because an incorrect certification decision carries financial and legal consequences. Acceptance rests on a golden dataset: cases previously answered manually become the reference the AI is measured against. Because the process itself was new, the evaluation method had to be designed as part of the programme. There was no benchmark to borrow.

Where Phase 2 stands

Client approval has been received. The scope is final, procurement is in progress and hiring of the team is under way. Development starts once that is done.

Expected outcomes

Targets, not results: fewer first-pass rejections (from roughly 30 to 40 percent towards under 5 percent), time saved per application, and measured accuracy against the golden dataset.

08 · What I carry forward

Sequencing is a product decision

Holding AI back a phase can be the thing that makes it adoptable. The question is not whether the model is good enough but whether the people accountable for the output can yet tell.

Where a process is genuinely new, the evaluation method is part of the deliverable. Designing the acceptance criteria alongside the thing being accepted is slower, and it is the only way the result means anything.
