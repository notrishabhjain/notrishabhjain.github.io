Certifying green hydrogen for the whole country, from a blank page

There was no process for certifying green hydrogen in India, so I started by defining one with the ministry. We then built the portal around it and passed an external security audit, and the Minister launched it on 1 July 2026. It has issued 8 certificates so far.

01 · Situation

There was no as-is process to digitise

The Green Hydrogen Certification of India scheme sits under the National Green Hydrogen Mission at MNRE, and its rules were issued as an Office Memorandum on 29 April 2025. India had no established digital certification process for green hydrogen of this kind, so there was no mature process to take and automate.

The scheme certifies hydrogen in four stages: a Concept Certificate, a Facility Certificate, a Provisional Certificate and a Final Certificate. Producers apply, accredited verifiers (ACVs) audit, and a Technical Committee (TC) decides. A Super Admin and the ministry run the registry, and anyone can verify a certificate without logging in.

Process definition, product design, delivery, stakeholder coordination, security testing and the later AI design all had to happen together rather than in sequence.

02 · The constraint

Define it, build it and pass the audit, all at once

  • No existing certification process, and so no benchmark to build against
  • Four certificate types, each needing an auditable record of every amendment
  • A fixed public launch, with the cloud environment arriving only two and a half weeks before the first UAT target
  • An external security audit by a CERT-In empanelled auditor standing between the build and production
  • Validation rules that change through office memoranda, not through releases
  • Verifiers and committee members who had never operated the digital process they were about to be measured on
03 · My mandate

From process definition to a Phase 2 AI scope

I started on the programme in January 2026 as Project Manager, leading a team of ten that included two business analysts, three full-stack developers, a tech lead, QA, UI/UX and DevOps. I owned delivery from process definition through production, ran the client relationship and change control, and wrote the scope for the Phase 2 AI layer.

The reports I wrote kept my own decisions and coordination separate from what the team built. This page does the same.

04 · Timeline

January to a public launch on 1 July

Between the milestones, the build ran as two-week sprints, about nine of them, with a demo every three. Sprint two built the database schema and registration. Sprint three built the Concept and Facility flows. Sprint four built the Provisional and Final flows, the rules engine and the UAT preparation.

07 · Workflow and rules

The scheme, enforced by the system

The platform has 11 modules, among them authentication and role-based access, the application forms, the workflow engine, ACV and TC review, the certificate registry, public verification, notifications, administration and an immutable audit trail. The workflow is a state machine, and every move has a named actor.

08 · Decisions

Three calls, each with its cost

Decision 1. Keep every certificate version; never overwrite one.

WhyA certificate is a legal artefact. If an amendment overwrites the original, the evidence of how the decision was reached is destroyed. So an amendment becomes a new record while the original stays intact, across all four certificate types. Concept and Facility certificates, for example, can be amended for an expansion, an ownership transfer or a new unit.

Trade-off acceptedAppend-only costs storage, makes every read path more complex, and means the interface has to explain which version a user is looking at. It buys a trail that survives a challenge to a certification years later.

Decision 2. Run an internal security review before the external audit.

WhyIf the external audit is the moment issues surface, the production date sits at the mercy of a report. The programme reviewed the code internally and fixed things in parallel, so the external audit met a platform that had already been attacked.

Trade-off acceptedIt pulled security effort forward into a window already competing with feature delivery, and some of that effort would have been unnecessary had the audit been lenient. It produced a short fix-and-revalidate round instead of a long remediation cycle.

Decision 3. Keep AI out of Phase 1, deliberately.

WhyAuditors needed to understand and operate the underlying process before they could judge an assistant working on top of it. An AI suggestion that nobody can evaluate is one nobody should trust. The decision was taken with the client on 19 March.

Trade-off acceptedIt delayed the efficiency gain by a full phase and meant launching with manual effort we knew could be reduced. It meant Phase 2 could be accepted on evidence rather than on faith.

09 · Architecture

How the system is put together

The platform runs as four deployable services behind a single load balancer: the front end, an authentication service, a user and application service, and an admin and workflow service. They share a versioned internal library. Behind them sit PostgreSQL, Redis for sessions and caching, a message broker for notifications, and object storage for documents and certificates.

Traffic passes a web application firewall with managed rule sets, and secrets and encryption keys sit in a managed vault. There were four environments (development, QA, UAT and production). Every change reached them through the same pipeline: a pull request, peer review, a CI build with a static-analysis quality gate, then a signed-off release.

  1. Pull requestpeer review
  2. CI buildquality gate
  3. Image registryversioned
  4. Sign-offeight roles
  5. Release8 to 11 pm window
  6. Smoke testthen rollback ready

The release window was 8 to 11 pm, after a database backup, with a rollback plan sized at 45 to 60 minutes. Eight roles signed each deployment: development, QA, security, DevOps and four others.

10 · Testing and security

Two rounds of user testing, and one external security audit

UAT ran in two rounds. Round one began on 10 May with test accounts for producers and verifiers. Round two was a workshop at the client venue on 9 June with producers, ACV agencies and the Technical Committee. The environment was reset to a clean state before it, and every P0 and P1 defect was closed before the workshop began. In all, 262 defects were logged through UAT, and by the June review only a handful of low-severity or deferred items remained open.

Security ran as a delivery stream. The internal review found 51 issues, and 48 were closed before the external audit opened. The external audit by a CERT-In empanelled agency then took one round, and every finding was closed. Its three reports arrived on 19 June and the fixes were back on 22 June, three days later. Clearance followed on 30 June.

Controls the programme added

  • Ownership and role checks on every record route
  • Short-lived tokens with rotating refresh tokens, and account lockout
  • Rate limits on sensitive endpoints, and constant-time checks on one-time passwords
  • Secrets and keys held in a vault, with encryption at rest and TLS in transit
  • An immutable audit log of actors, actions and outcomes
  • A web application firewall in front of everything

The finding types and infrastructure detail stay off this page by design.

The guardrails

No personal data or credentials in a prompt. Every output reviewed by a person. The developer owns every merged change. Phase 1 contains no AI feature at all. The wider method is on the Method page.

14 · Phase 2

Phase 2: AI, with two rules attached

I wrote the scope for Phase 2. It covers six modules: document intelligence on submissions, emissions anomaly detection, a verification assistant for auditors, application summaries for the committee, compliance pattern intelligence, and process-risk prediction.

AI advises. A named officer approves every consequential action.

Every generated claim cites the document supporting it.

Neither guardrail is negotiable, because an incorrect certification decision carries financial and legal consequences. Acceptance rests on a golden dataset: cases previously answered manually become the reference the AI is measured against. Because the process itself was new, the evaluation method had to be designed as part of the programme. There was no benchmark to borrow.

Where Phase 2 stands

The request for quotation is approved and procurement is in progress. The plan is six months from award, with two vendor resources working under me. The vendor builds only after procurement completes.

Expected outcomes

Targets, not results: fewer first-pass rejections (from roughly 30 to 40 percent towards under 5 percent), time saved per application, and measured accuracy against the golden dataset.

15 · What I carry forward

Sequencing is a product decision

Holding AI back a phase can be the thing that makes it adoptable. The question is not whether the model is good enough but whether the people accountable for the output can yet tell.

Where a process is genuinely new, the evaluation method is part of the deliverable. Designing the acceptance criteria alongside the thing being accepted is slower, and it is the only way the result means anything.

Change control is worth the three days. The change request that touched the workflow still went through impact analysis, a BRD update and client approval, and that is why the registry's record of what it does stayed true.
