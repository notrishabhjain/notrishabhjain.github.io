Green Hydrogen Certification of India

01 — Situation

There was no as-is process to digitise

The Green Hydrogen Certification of India scheme sits under the National Green Hydrogen Mission at MNRE. India did not have an established digital certification process for green hydrogen of this nature, which meant there was no mature process to take and automate.

So process definition, product design, technology delivery, stakeholder coordination, security certification and the future AI design all had to happen together rather than in sequence.

02 — The constraint

Define it, build it, and pass the audit — concurrently

  • No pre-existing certification process, and therefore no benchmark to build against
  • Four certificate types, each needing an auditable record of every amendment
  • A CERT-In empanelled security audit standing between the build and production
  • Validation rules that change through office memoranda, not through releases
  • Auditors who had never operated the digital process they were about to be measured on

03 — My mandate

Lead project delivery and UX, translate the emerging certification process into a production-ready national digital registry, and author the Phase 2 AI scope.

04 — Decisions

Decision 01

Design the registry append-only.

WhyA certificate is a legal artefact. If an amendment overwrites the original, the evidence of how the decision was reached is destroyed. An amendment therefore becomes a new record while the original stays intact, across all four certificate types.

Trade-off acceptedAppend-only costs storage, makes every read path more complex, and means the interface has to explain which version a user is looking at. It buys a trail that survives a challenge to a certification years later.

Decision 02

Run an internal audit before the external one.

WhyLetting a CERT-In empanelled audit be the moment issues surface puts the production date at the mercy of a report. The programme pre-audited internally and remediated in parallel, so the external audit met a platform that had already been attacked.

Trade-off acceptedIt pulled security effort forward into a window already competing with feature delivery, and some of that effort would have been unnecessary had the external audit been lenient. It produced a single-pass result instead of a remediation cycle.

Decision 03

Keep AI out of Phase 1 deliberately.

WhyAuditors needed to understand and operate the underlying process before they could meaningfully judge an assistant working on top of it. An AI suggestion nobody can evaluate is an AI suggestion nobody should trust.

Trade-off acceptedIt delayed the efficiency gain by a full phase, and meant launching with manual effort we knew could be reduced. It meant Phase 2 could be accepted on evidence rather than on faith.

05 — Delivery

Security as a delivery stream, not a gate at the end

48 of 51 findings were closed during internal pre-audit before the CERT-In empanelled audit opened, and the platform subsequently passed in a single pass.

The three remaining findings came from customised reusable components. They were manually verified and the residual risk was signed by the CTO rather than quietly carried — which is the difference between an accepted risk and an unowned one.

06 — Outcome

A national green hydrogen certification registry went live in June, with producers registering on the same public launch date.

The AI layer, and the two guardrails on it

I authored 80–90% of the Phase 2 AI scope — approximately ₹2 crore over about six months, spanning data scientists through testers: submission-gap analysis, validation against rules that change through office memoranda, auditor checklist pre-fill, emissions reconciliation, and evidence-backed assistance throughout.

AI advises. A named officer approves every consequential action.
Every generated claim cites the document supporting it.

Neither guardrail is negotiable, because an incorrect certification decision carries financial and legal consequences. Acceptance rests on a golden dataset: cases previously answered manually become the reference the AI is measured against. Because the process itself was new, the evaluation methodology had to be designed as part of the programme — there was no benchmark to borrow.

07 — What I'd carry forward

Sequencing is a product decision, and holding AI back a phase can be the thing that makes it adoptable. The question is not whether the model is good enough but whether the people accountable for the output can yet tell.

And where a process is genuinely new, the evaluation method is part of the deliverable. Designing acceptance criteria alongside the thing being accepted is slower, and it is the only way the result means anything.
