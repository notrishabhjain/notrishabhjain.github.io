From a review spreadsheet to a live case platform in about a month

A statutory commission was reviewing submissions in an Excel workbook, with no routing between officers and no audit trail. I led delivery of the platform that replaced it, took it live about a month after kick-off, and have kept it shipping since.

The situation

The commission receives memoranda from employee bodies and government departments. Each memorandum carries many demands, and the demand is the unit the commission decides on. Memoranda arrived as an export from a citizen-participation platform and were reviewed in an Excel workbook, with no routing between officers and no record of who had done what.

The commission works to a fixed term, so the go-live date was fixed too. The platform had to be built in about a month on open-source technology, go through an external security audit, and go live through four parties that do not report to each other: the client, a cloud provider, a government network agency and a certificate authority.

What I did as project manager

1. Cut the plan from five sprints to two.

Two-week sprints carrying 41 stories, with 12 milestones, 7 client dependencies, 8 tracked risks and 13 done-criteria, so that no single module could move the date.

Trade-offA compressed plan put more coordination on me in the middle of delivery.

2. Turned the workbook into a governed workflow.

Every demand moves through defined stages with a named holder. Any officer can close an invalid item for one of three recorded reasons, a restored demand restarts at examination, and the action history and audit log cannot be edited. Duplicate checking in this phase is deterministic text matching, so the commission could be told exactly how it works.

Trade-offOfficers had to learn a process instead of a file, and re-worded duplicates can slip past an exact check.

3. Ran the go-live dependency chain across four parties.

When browsers rejected the new certificate in the last days before go-live, I kept a dated timeline, wrote the escalation notes to the network agency, and kept the root cause marked “not confirmed” until a check proved it, after a first diagnosis was overturned.

Trade-offHonest status reporting meant saying “we do not know yet” to senior stakeholders.

4. Kept shipping after launch, and made the backlog honest.

Three requests from a client review after go-live (grouping repeated demands, an in-portal loop for ministry questions and replies, and department-wise nodal officers) went live in 16 to 43 days. I had 24 builds reconciled into one 109-item backlog, which surfaced 11 delivered items not yet promoted, an unmerged backend branch, and a missing-fonts defect that was rebuilt and fixed the same day.

Trade-offScope grew after the as-built specification; each request was recorded as a decision and fed into the next phase’s requirements.

My role

What I owned

The plan, scope, dependencies and risks; requirements and workflow rules; governance and escalations across four parties; approvals and reports; release tracking and the backlog; and the AI evaluation.

What the team delivered

Design, build, testing and DevOps, the data load, and 24 production builds.

What the client decided

The commission’s rules, the changes requested after launch, and approvals for hosting and the security audit.

The outcome

The platform went live about a month after kick-off, with 143 documented requirements across 11 modules and 21 screens, after an external security audit run in a fixed window before go-live. Since then it has shipped 24 production builds and 86 of 109 backlog items, added an in-portal ministry loop and grouping of repeated demands, and carried the commission’s caseload through examination and review.

AI in this programme: measured before trusted

Evaluated · advisory onlyNext phase gated on where inference runs

The next phase adds AI that drafts demands from a memorandum and flags near-duplicates. Before anyone relied on it, I had it evaluated against the officers’ own work. It reproduced 64% of officer-created demands (about 72% counting borderline matches) while creating 1.7 times as many, and on a 27-case duplicate test 20 cases failed, including different asks scored as duplicates.

So the AI stays advisory: it suggests, the officer decides, nothing is applied automatically, and no text leaves the government environment. What gates the next phase is a decision, not code: where AI inference will run.

Where it stands now

Live · in operations

The platform is live and in daily use by the commission’s officers. I run the release tracking, the backlog and change requests with the client, and the AI phase waits on the inference decision.

What I carry forward

A first diagnosis is a hypothesis until a check confirms it, and status reports should say so.

Track what is deployed, not only what is built. Reconciling builds with the backlog found finished work that users could not yet see.

Measure AI against the people it will assist before anyone relies on it. A 64% match is a reason to keep a person in the loop, not a reason to ship.
