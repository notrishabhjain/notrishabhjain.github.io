Case study · Statutory commission · Central government

Live in about a month, against a date fixed in law

A statutory commission was running its caseload from a spreadsheet. I led delivery of the platform that replaced it: live in about a month, with four organisations coordinating and none of them reporting to the others.

The challenge

A statutory commission examines submissions from government departments and employee bodies and turns them into recommendations. When I picked up the engagement, its caseload sat in a shared spreadsheet, with no record of who held a case or what had been decided on it.

The commission's term is fixed in law, so the go-live date could not move. The platform had to run on open-source tools in a government cloud, use the government's single sign-on and pass an external security audit. Four organisations had to deliver it together, and none of them reports to another.

What I did

1. Re-planned the delivery around the date.

A slip in any one module would have moved the whole date. I cut the plan from five sprints to two and ran the modules as parallel streams. Schedule risk moved into coordination, which I could manage, and away from the deadline, which nobody could.

2. Set up governance that four organisations would accept.

With no reporting line to lean on, the project ran through institutional channels on a fixed rhythm: a weekly steering committee, one status report listing every dependency with its owner and next step, and prototype walkthroughs with agency and commission leadership so that decisions came in days.

3. Turned the spreadsheet into an accountable workflow.

The officers' own workbook became the first requirements document. Every case now moves through defined stages, with a named officer at each step and an audit log that cannot be edited, so anyone can see who holds a case and what happened to it.

4. Kept the risky streams off the critical path.

Data from more than a dozen offices, with no common format, was loaded as its own stream. The external security audit was planned into the final week from the first day. Neither could hold up the build.

My role

What I owned

The delivery plan and the move to parallel streams; scope, schedule, risks and dependencies; requirements and workflow rules; governance and reporting across four organisations; and the scope of the next-stage AI layer.

What the team delivered

Design, build and testing of 11 modules, and the one-time data load. A team of eight, including me.

What the client decided

The commission's rules and approvals, the hosting, network and identity arrangements run by other bodies, and security clearance.

The result

The platform went live about a month after kick-off, on the date set at the start, after passing an external security audit. Officers sign in through the government's single sign-on, data from more than a dozen offices now sits in one place, and officers enter about twenty times as many cases a day as before. It is sized for about ten times today's volume, so no rebuild is needed during the commission's term.

Where AI fits

AI layer · scoped, next stage

I scoped the next stage: AI that helps officers spot duplicate cases and find the evidence a case refers to. It only advises. A named officer makes every decision.

The lesson

When a date cannot move, decide on the first day which other variable takes the strain. Here it was coordination effort, and naming it early made the rest of the plan honest.

The best first requirement was the spreadsheet the officers already used. Each of its columns became a proper field on the platform.

01 · Situation

A statutory commission still running on a spreadsheet

The commission receives submissions from government departments and employee bodies, examines each point they raise, and places its recommendations. When I picked up the engagement, that work ran on a shared workbook, and the commission's term is fixed in law.

The platform had to give officers a governed workflow, take in data from more than a dozen offices that shared no common format, and go live while four separate bodies coordinated the work.

02 · The constraint

The date could not move, so something else had to give

On most projects, when requirements churn or a dependency slips, the date moves. Here it could not. Every other variable had to take the strain: the shape of the scope, the sequencing, the team structure and the way coordination ran.

  • A go-live date fixed by the commission's term
  • A spreadsheet to replace with an accountable workflow
  • Volumes expected to grow many times within months of launch
  • Four organisations with no reporting line between them
  • Open-source technology, government-cloud hosting, single sign-on and an external security audit as conditions of go-live
03 · My mandate

Deliver it, define it, then scope what comes next

I joined as Project Manager at the start of delivery and led a team of eight. I owned the plan, scope, schedule, risks and dependencies, the requirements and workflow definition, and coordination with the commission and the three other bodies. After launch I led the move towards an AI-assisted examination layer and wrote its scope.

05 · Governance and stakeholders

Four bodies, no reporting line, one date

Across four organisations that do not report to each other, the only levers are institutional, not managerial. So coordination ran through the proper channels, on a rhythm everyone could plan around.

  • A weekly steering committee where open decisions were taken, not just reported
  • One status report to the commission's leadership, listing every dependency with its owner, status and next step
  • Prototype walkthroughs with agency and commission leadership, so feedback arrived in days rather than in review cycles
  • A progress review presented to senior civil-service leadership during delivery
  • Escalation through the right channel, run alongside diagnosis rather than instead of it
06 · Decisions

Three calls, each with its cost

Decision 1. Parallelise the delivery rather than sequence it.

WhySequencing the modules would have put every slip straight onto the fixed date. Running them as parallel streams across two sprints moved schedule risk into coordination, which I could manage, and away from the deadline, which I could not. The original plan had five sprints, and I cut it to two.

Trade-off acceptedParallel streams cost more coordination and more of my own time in the middle of the project, and with a team of eight that was real. It bought a delivery shape where no single module could sink the date.

Decision 2. Turn the spreadsheet into a proper workflow, not a nicer spreadsheet.

WhyA shared file has no state, no owner and no audit trail, so work stalls without anyone seeing it. I specified each case as a set of stages, from creation through examination, an optional department referral and senior review to placement for decision, with role-based access for every officer.

Trade-off acceptedIt is slower than a spreadsheet for a single quick edit, and it asked officers to learn a process rather than a file. That is the price of being able to say who holds a case and what happened to it.

Decision 3. Build for month six, not launch day.

WhyVolumes were expected to grow many times within months. Designing for launch-day volume would have meant a rebuild inside the same fixed term.

Trade-off acceptedDesigning for roughly ten times the live volume spent effort on load the platform did not yet carry, which is hard to justify against a four-week clock. It avoided a re-architecture during the commission's term, when there would have been no room for one.

08 · Delivery and quality

Built, tested and audited inside four weeks

The first week went on freezing requirements and scope and setting up environments and single sign-on. The build then ran in two parallel streams, and the final week held integration testing, user acceptance testing, training, the external security audit, the data load and go-live. The stack was deliberately conventional and open source, because the risk was the date, not the technology.

Access is by role, and the action history and audit log are append-only, enforced in the database itself. The platform carries more than 700 automated checks.

How the delivery used AI

The project ran as an AI-assisted lifecycle: AI support in discovery, requirements, planning, reporting and documentation, with a person accountable for every output. The method is set out on How I work.

The platform is built for much larger volumes than it holds today, and it stays within the government's requirements for open-source technology and government-cloud hosting.

The AI layer, and what it is not allowed to do

Scoped, not builtNot part of the live platform

I wrote the scope for the AI examination layer: duplicate detection, so that the same point arriving through several bodies is recognised as one; anomaly detection; evidence matching against the submissions a case cites; and retrieval of the referenced sources. The aim is that officers stop reading every page to find the one that matters. It is not to automate the examination.

Duplicates are graded by similarity into identical, near and related matches. The layer is advisory only, separate from the live platform, and planned as a two-month delivery.

11 · What I carry forward

Decide early what gives when the date will not

Where a date is genuinely immovable, the useful question is not how to go faster but which variable is allowed to absorb the risk. Naming that early, here coordination effort, is what makes the rest of the plan honest.

Escalation and diagnosis are parallel tracks, not a sequence. Raising an issue through the right channel does not mean waiting for it.

Read what is already built before proposing what is new. Several of the places AI was wrong here were places where the as-built specification already had the answer.
