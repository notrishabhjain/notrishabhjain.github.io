A national pay-review platform, live in 30 days

India’s 8th Central Pay Commission was tracking demands from ministries and staff unions in a spreadsheet. With a statutory deadline and four organisations that didn’t report to one another, my team replaced it with an audited, workflow-driven platform in about a month.

Context

The situation

The 8th Central Pay Commission reviews salaries and pensions for about 7 million central government employees and pensioners in India. Ministries, staff associations and federations submit demands to it, and each one has to be examined, referred, reviewed and placed before the Commission.

When I picked the project up, those demands lived in an Excel sheet. Nobody could see who held a demand or what had been decided. The Commission’s term is fixed by law, so the go-live date could not slip, and four organisations — the Commission, the national e-governance division, the cloud provider and the identity service — had to move together without any of them reporting to the others.

Constraints

What couldn’t move

Deadline fixed by statuteExternal security audit before go-liveOpen-source components onlyGovernment cloud and single sign-onFour organisations, no shared reporting lineVolume expected to grow 10× within months
Key decisions

The calls I made, and what each one cost

Compress five sprints into two parallel streams

Any single module running late would have moved the date. Building the modules side by side meant no one module sat on the critical path.

Trade-off acceptedMore coordination and a heavier load on me mid-project. I named that cost on day one so it was planned for, not discovered.

Turn the spreadsheet into an auditable workflow

Each demand now moves through fixed stages — created, examined, optionally referred to a ministry, reviewed by a senior officer, placed before the Commission. Every step has a named owner and an entry that can’t be edited afterwards.

Trade-off acceptedSlower than typing into a cell, and officers had to learn a process instead of a file. In return, anyone can see who holds a demand and why it moved.

Use the existing sheet as the first requirement

Its 24 columns became typed fields, and data from 14 offices that shared no common format was loaded once into one place.

Trade-off acceptedSome columns carried habits rather than rules, so each one was challenged before it became a field.

Size for month six, not launch day

The platform was designed for about 200,000 demands — roughly ten times what it holds today — so there would be no re-architecture inside the Commission’s term.

Trade-off acceptedMore design time inside a 30-day window.

My role

Who did what

What I owned

The delivery plan and the switch to parallel streams, workflow rules, coordination across four organisations, status reporting, and the scope of the AI phase.

What the team delivered

Build and test of 11 modules and the one-time data load, by a team of eight including me.

What the client decided

The Commission’s rules and approvals, hosting, network and identity arrangements run by other bodies, and the security audit verdict.

Results

What changed

  • Live on 24 August 2026, about a month after kick-off, after passing an external security audit (19–21 August).
  • ~21,000 demands managed by around 200 officers, signing in through government single sign-on.
  • Data entry up from ~50 to ~1,000 demands a day.
  • Data from 14 offices with no shared format now sits in one system with a full audit trail.
Where AI fits

AI with a named owner

The next phase adds AI that flags likely duplicate demands and retrieves the evidence a demand cites. It advises only: a named officer makes every decision.

Takeaway

What I carry forward

When a date can’t move, decide on day one which other thing will take the strain — here, coordination effort — and plan for it openly. The best first requirement is the spreadsheet people already use.
