01 — Situation

A directory of portals, not a service

The objective was to turn a static directory of disconnected government portals into a single digital front door, federating more than twenty sources: One District One Product, iGOT, e-Courts, MyScheme, the services portal, Aadhaar, income-tax verification, tourism, government directories and live news.

02 — The constraint

Twenty-two languages, and a source ecosystem nothing off the shelf could ingest

Two constraints shaped the build. The sources were heterogeneous enough that an off-the-shelf ingestion model could not adequately handle them. And the platform had to work in 22 languages, where a plausible-sounding mistranslation of a government service is worse than no translation at all.

03 — My mandate

Work from the PMO side on platform strategy, product definition, and the language quality system.

04 — Decisions

Decision 01

Treat translation as a measurable quality problem, not a model choice.

WhyRather than selecting a translation engine and accepting its output, the programme instrumented quality: machine translation, two reviewers per language at launch, corrections fed back into the model, language-specific terminology, and native-speaker intervention where it was needed.

Trade-off acceptedTwo reviewers per language across 22 languages is a standing cost, and it slowed launch. It produced a number we could manage against — an initial error rate near one sentence in ten, with roughly 80% of output accepted as translated.

Decision 02

Graduate review by traffic and measured quality.

WhyReviewing every language equally forever would have been unaffordable and wrong: a high-traffic language with a poor error rate needs more attention than a low-traffic one performing well. Review intensity was graduated on both signals.

Trade-off acceptedIt accepted that lower-traffic languages would get less scrutiny, which is a real quality trade-off and had to be stated rather than hidden. It made the review model sustainable past launch.

Decision 03

Build a custom CMS rather than adopt an ingestion product.

WhyThe heterogeneous source ecosystem could not be adequately handled by an off-the-shelf ingestion model, so the programme specified a custom CMS and then semantic search, recommendations and summarisation on top of it.

Trade-off acceptedCustom means owning maintenance and upgrades that a product vendor would otherwise carry. It was the only way to federate sources that did not agree on structure.

05 — Delivery

The language pipeline

  • Machine translation
  • Two reviewers per language
  • Correction feedback
  • Terminology
  • Graduated review

Corrections were not just fixes: they were training signal and terminology, which is why the error rate was something the programme could move rather than simply report.

06 — Outcome

20+federated sources behind one door
22languages live
~80%of machine output accepted as translated
~1 in 10initial sentence error rate

A single front door over twenty-plus federated sources, with a language quality system that could be measured and improved, and semantic search, recommendations and summarisation specified on a custom CMS for the following phase.

07 — What I'd carry forward

AI quality is an operating process, not a model selection. The reviewers, the feedback loop, the terminology and the graduation rule were the product; the model was a component.

Instrumenting quality before scaling is what made the trade-offs explicit. Without a measured error rate there is no honest way to decide which languages get less review.
