Case study · National clean-energy mission · Central government

A national certification platform, from a blank page

A new national scheme needed a way to certify clean-energy production, and no process existed. I led the work to define that process with the ministry first, then deliver the platform around it: built, security-audited and launched publicly on the date set.

The challenge

A national clean-energy mission had set production targets, and for production to count, someone has to check and certify how it was made. The scheme rules had been issued. What did not exist was a way to apply, audit, approve and issue certificates online.

There was no existing process to copy, so the process had to be defined before anything could be built. The launch date was fixed, the cloud environment arrived only two and a half weeks before the first testing target, and an external security audit stood between the build and going live.

What I did

1. Defined the process with the ministry first.

Starting from the scheme rules, the team mapped every user journey, studied comparable portals and presented the process to the client. Nothing went to development until the client accepted it. About 250 features across 11 modules were baselined and signed before the first sprint, and every later change went through a formal change request.

2. Re-planned around a late dependency, and held the date.

When the cloud environment arrived weeks late, I re-cut the plan with the real dates and released user testing in phases instead of waiting for the whole build. The launch date did not move.

3. Ran security as a delivery stream, not a gate at the end.

The team reviewed its own code first and closed over 90% of its findings before the external audit began. The audit then needed one short round of fixes, and clearance came the day before launch.

4. Launched without AI, and scoped it for the next phase.

The officers who verify and approve certificates had never used this process. Phase 1 launched without AI so they could learn it first. I wrote the Phase 2 AI scope and the procurement document for it.

My role

What I owned

The process definition and requirements baseline, the plan and its re-cuts, change control, delivery governance and escalation, the user-testing and security-remediation plan, and the Phase 2 scope and request for quotation.

What the team delivered

Design, build and testing of 11 modules, and the fixes that came out of the security reviews. A team of ten, including me.

What the client decided

The scheme rules, sign-off of the baseline and of each change, acceptance after user testing, security clearance, and the decision to launch.

The result

The platform launched publicly on the date set. Producers, verification agencies and committee members all work on it, applications are flowing and the first certificates have been issued. Digital signing, notifications and the document locker all work.

After launch, the first change request touched the workflow itself. It went through impact analysis, a requirements update and client approval, and was ready in about three days.

Where AI fits

Phase 2 · approved, in procurement

Phase 2 adds AI to check submissions, flag unusual emissions data and support verifiers, under two rules. AI advises, and a named officer approves every important decision. Every claim it makes must point to the document behind it. It will be accepted against cases that people have already answered by hand.

The lesson

When a project has to define its own process, the time spent agreeing it with the client before development is what makes the launch date possible.

When a process is new, the people who must judge the result have to understand it first. Holding AI back by one phase means Phase 2 can be accepted on evidence.

01 · Situation

There was no process to digitise

The certification scheme sits under a national clean-energy mission, and its rules had been issued by the ministry. There was no established digital certification process of this kind, so there was nothing mature to take and automate.

The scheme certifies production in four stages: a Concept Certificate, a Facility Certificate, a Provisional Certificate and a Final Certificate. Producers apply, accredited verification agencies audit, and a technical committee decides. An administrator and the ministry run the registry, and anyone can verify a certificate without logging in.

Process definition, product design, delivery, stakeholder coordination, security testing and the later AI design all had to happen together rather than in sequence.

02 · The constraint

Define it, build it and pass the audit, all at once

  • No existing certification process, and so no benchmark to build against
  • Four certificate types, each needing an auditable record of every amendment
  • A fixed public launch, with the cloud environment arriving only two and a half weeks before the first testing target
  • An external security audit by a government-empanelled auditor standing between the build and production
  • Validation rules that change through official notices, not through releases
  • Verifiers and committee members who had never operated the digital process they were about to be measured on
03 · My mandate

From process definition to a Phase 2 AI scope

I started at the mandate as Project Manager, leading a team of ten: two business analysts, three full-stack developers, a tech lead, QA, UI/UX and DevOps. I owned delivery from process definition to production, ran the client relationship and change control, and wrote the scope for the Phase 2 AI layer.

The reports I wrote kept my own decisions and coordination separate from what the team built. This page does the same.

07 · Workflow and rules

The scheme, enforced by the system

The platform has 11 modules, among them role-based access, the application forms, the workflow engine, verifier and committee review, the certificate registry, public verification, notifications, administration and an immutable audit trail. The workflow is a state machine, and every move has a named actor.

Rules settled, and the reason for each

RuleWhy
A Provisional Certificate is issued automatically, in the same transaction as the submission, with no verifier or committee involvedThe scheme says so, and a review step would contradict it
When a Final Certificate exceeds the emissions threshold, every Provisional Certificate for that facility is withdrawn in one transactionA partial withdrawal would leave the registry in a state the scheme does not allow
A fee applies to the Final Certificate onlyThe scheme sets no fee for the earlier certificates and provides no refund
Every form carries two digital signature options, and a certificate is generated only after a valid signatureNon-repudiation
After a rejection, the committee appoints a second verifier, and its decision is finalThe scheme provides for exactly one second opinion
Verifier and committee accounts are approved by the implementing agency before they can actA control before anyone touches the workflow
Verifiers and producers talk through defined threads; there is no direct thread between committee and producerEvery question stays on the record
08 · Decisions

Three calls, each with its cost

Decision 1. Keep every certificate version; never overwrite one.

WhyA certificate is a legal record. If an amendment overwrites the original, the evidence of how the decision was reached is destroyed. So an amendment becomes a new record while the original stays intact, across all four certificate types.

Trade-off acceptedAppend-only costs storage, makes every read path more complex, and means the interface has to explain which version a user is looking at. It buys a trail that survives a challenge to a certification years later.

Decision 2. Run an internal security review before the external audit.

WhyIf the external audit is the moment issues surface, the production date sits at the mercy of a report. The team reviewed the code internally and fixed issues in parallel, so the external audit met a platform that had already been attacked.

Trade-off acceptedIt pulled security effort forward into a window already competing with feature delivery. It produced a short fix-and-revalidate round instead of a long remediation cycle.

Decision 3. Keep AI out of Phase 1, deliberately.

WhyVerifiers needed to understand and operate the underlying process before they could judge an assistant working on top of it. An AI suggestion that nobody can evaluate is one nobody should trust. The decision was taken with the client.

Trade-off acceptedIt delayed the efficiency gain by a full phase and meant launching with manual effort we knew could be reduced. It meant Phase 2 could be accepted on evidence rather than on faith.

09 · Testing and security

Two rounds of user testing, and one external security audit

User testing ran in two rounds. The first used test accounts for producers and verifiers. The second was a workshop at the client's venue with producers, verification agencies and the technical committee; the environment was reset to a clean state before it, and every critical and high defect was closed before it began. In all, 262 defects were logged through user testing, and by the final review only a handful of low-severity or deferred items remained open.

Security ran as a delivery stream. The internal review closed over 90% of its findings before the external audit opened, and the rest were verified by hand with the risk signed off by a named owner. The external audit then took one round: its reports came in and the fixes were back three days later, and clearance followed the day before launch.

Controls the team added

  • Ownership and role checks on every record route
  • Short-lived tokens with rotating refresh tokens, and account lockout
  • Rate limits on sensitive endpoints, and constant-time checks on one-time passwords
  • Secrets and keys held in a vault, with encryption at rest and in transit
  • An immutable audit log of actors, actions and outcomes
  • A web application firewall in front of everything

The finding types and infrastructure detail stay off this page by design.

11 · Change control

A formal flow, and a three-day turnaround

After launch, the client asked for changes. Each one went through the same change process, and it is the one I run rather than skip when a request is urgent.

  1. Requestfrom the client
  2. Impact analysismodules, rules, data
  3. Flow changeformally redrawn
  4. Requirements updatedannexure
  5. Client approvalbefore any build
  6. Build and testpull request, review
  7. Releasesigned off

The first change request changed date-based exceptions in the workflow, a payment step, how administrator credentials are created and a dashboard view. Because the workflow itself changed, it went through a formal flow change, impact assessment, a requirements update and client approval. It took about three days from the signed request to the change being ready. Claude helped with the impact analysis, the flow design, the exception rules, the code, the tests and the document updates, and I approved each artefact before it went to the client.

The guardrails

No personal data or credentials in a prompt. Every output reviewed by a person. The developer owns every merged change. Phase 1 contains no AI feature at all. The wider method is on How I work.

13 · Phase 2

Phase 2: AI, with two rules attached

I wrote the scope for Phase 2. It covers six modules: document intelligence on submissions, emissions anomaly detection, a verification assistant for auditors, application summaries for the committee, compliance pattern intelligence, and process-risk prediction.

AI advises. A named officer approves every consequential action.

Every generated claim cites the document supporting it.

Neither guardrail is negotiable, because an incorrect certification decision carries financial and legal consequences. Acceptance rests on a golden dataset: cases previously answered by hand become the reference the AI is measured against. Because the process itself was new, the evaluation method had to be designed as part of the programme. There was no benchmark to borrow.

Where Phase 2 stands

The request for quotation is approved and procurement is in progress. The plan is six months from award, with two vendor resources working under me. The vendor builds only after procurement completes.

Expected outcomes

Targets, not results: fewer first-pass rejections (from roughly 30 to 40 percent towards under 5 percent), time saved per application, and measured accuracy against the golden dataset.

14 · What I carry forward

Sequencing is a product decision

Holding AI back a phase can be the thing that makes it adoptable. The question is not whether the model is good enough but whether the people accountable for the output can yet tell.

Where a process is genuinely new, the evaluation method is part of the deliverable. Designing the acceptance criteria alongside the thing being accepted is slower, and it is the only way the result means anything.

Change control is worth the three days. The change request that touched the workflow still went through impact analysis, a requirements update and client approval, and that is why the registry's record of what it does stayed true.
