A loss-making unit, turned around by fixing the model before the headcount

About 55 applications supporting wind hardware under real 24×7 obligations. The problem was the operating model, not the people, and AI took on production work only as fast as the evidence allowed.

01 · Situation

A unit at 100% loss, supporting hardware that does not stop

I inherited an application organisation supporting a wind portfolio at a complete loss, staffed at roughly twice the contractual requirement, with genuine round-the-clock obligations. Turbines do not stop at close of business, and neither can the applications watching them.

The scope was about 55 applications for a global wind-energy OEM. The client is not named here.

02 · The constraint

The obvious fix was the wrong one

The standard response to a loss-making unit at twice its required staffing is to cut. That would have removed the cost and left the cause, because the cause was spread across six things at once.

Operating modelKnowledge managementSLA definitionAutomationCommercial scopeTeam structure

Critical knowledge lived inside individual senior engineers. Support, enhancements and environment changes were measured as one blended pool, which hid where the service was actually failing. Out-of-scope work was absorbed rather than charged.

03 · My mandate

Own the whole unit

I owned the P&L, delivery, operational performance, product responsibilities and people management for the application organisation, as Project Manager, Product Owner and Scrum Master.

04 · Decisions

Four calls, each with its cost

Decision 1. Fix the operating model before reducing people.

WhyCutting first removes capacity and keeps the dependency: the work still needs doing and only a few people can do it. I standardised SOPs so knowledge stopped living in individuals, shifted repetitive work left, and established an L1.5 layer. Only then did I reduce headcount.

Trade-off acceptedIt delayed the visible financial improvement by months while the cost base stayed where it was, which is an uncomfortable thing to hold in front of a P&L review. It made the reduction survivable instead of a transfer of risk onto the people who remained.

  1. Standardise the knowledgeSOPs, not individuals
  2. Move work leftrepetitive work to the right tier
  3. Add an L1.5 layerbetween first line and engineers
  4. Then resizelast, not first

Decision 2. Separate the commercial signals.

WhySupport, enhancements and environment changes were measured as one service pool, so failures in one averaged out against successes in another. Each got its own commitment, which made the real failure visible and chargeable.

Trade-off acceptedThree commitments are harder to report and to govern than one blended number, and it removed a convenient place to hide. That was the point.

Decision 3. Commercialise scope instead of absorbing it.

WhyOut-of-scope requests were being delivered for free, which hid demand and funded the loss. They became separately scoped work, generating around $200,000 a year of new business.

Trade-off acceptedIt introduced friction into requests that used to just happen, and it meant holding a line with a client used to getting them. It turned a leak into revenue and made the real demand visible.

Decision 4. Give AI production authority only as fast as the evidence allowed.

WhyAI entered production support in controlled stages: shadow mode, then a six-hour window, then full-day operation, with each expansion resting on evidence from the stage before. The environment combined rule-based scripting with agentic AI and AIOps on an internal platform. It reached production, measured by successful ticket closure and routing, and no false positives were detected.

Trade-off acceptedStaging meant running human and machine in parallel for longer than strictly necessary, paying twice for the same coverage during shadow. It is why the expansion was never reversed.

05 · Delivery

When the safety alerting failed with technicians on a turbine

A safety-alerting service used by field technicians failed overnight while two technicians were on a turbine. I raised it as a P1 and activated the on-call rota. The fault was traced to a third-party regional outage outside our application stack.

Outside our stack did not make it outside our responsibility. Until the provider restored service, the team kept manual all-clear controls in place and sanitised the affected regions before the incident was closed.

07 · What I carry forward

Reduce people last

A unit losing money at twice the required headcount looks like a staffing problem and usually is not. Standardise the knowledge, move the work to the right tier, then resize. Reversing that order transfers the risk to whoever is left.

A blended metric is a place for failure to hide. Separating the commitments cost reporting simplicity and bought the ability to see what was actually wrong.
