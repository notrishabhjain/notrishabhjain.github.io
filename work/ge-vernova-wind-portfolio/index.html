Case study 03 · GE Vernova · Project Manager, Product Owner, Scrum Master · 2024–26

GE Vernova Wind Portfolio

A loss-making application unit supporting wind hardware under genuine 24×7 obligations, turned net-positive in six months by changing the operating model before the headcount.

  • Loss-making unit
  • Model before headcount
  • Staged AI authority
  • 12–15% profit

01 — Context

A unit at a complete loss, supporting hardware that runs 24×7

I inherited an application organisation supporting a wind portfolio at 100% loss, staffed at roughly twice the contractual requirement, under genuine round-the-clock operational obligations — the hardware does not stop at close of business, and neither can the applications that watch it.

Scope covered roughly 50 to 55 applications and a team of about 25 people.

02 — Challenge

The problem was not the headcount

The obvious move on a loss-making unit is to cut people. That would have removed the cost and kept the cause, because the cause was spread across six things at once:

  • Operating model
  • Knowledge management
  • SLA definition
  • Automation
  • Commercial scope
  • Team structure

Critical knowledge lived inside individual senior engineers. Support, enhancements and environment changes were measured as one blended service pool, which hid where the service was actually failing. Out-of-scope work was absorbed rather than charged.

03 — Mandate

Own P&L, delivery, operational performance, product responsibilities and people management for the application organisation — as Project Manager, Product Owner and Scrum Master.

04 — Critical decisions

Decision 01

Fix the operating model before reducing people.Why. SOPs were standardised first, so critical knowledge stopped living in individual senior resources. Repetitive work was shifted left and an L1.5 layer was established — and only then was headcount reduced. Cutting first would have removed the capacity and kept the dependency.

Decision 02

Separate the commercial signals.Why. Support, enhancements and environment changes were given separate commitments rather than being measured as one blended pool. You cannot manage a service whose failures average out against each other.

Decision 03

Commercialise scope instead of absorbing it.Why. Out-of-scope requests became separately scoped work, generating around $200,000 a year of new business from demand the unit had previously been giving away.

05 — Applied AI

AI earned its authority one stage at a time

AI entered production support through controlled stages, and each expansion of authority was based on evidence from the stage before it — not on a decision to trust it.

  • Shadow mode
  • Six-hour window
  • Full-day operation

The environment combined rule-based scripting, agentic AI and AIOps on an internal platform. The result is visible in the operating pattern rather than in a model metric: automation reached 70–80% and weekends now run without a human rota.

07 — Critical incident leadership

Two technicians on a turbine, and the safety alerting down

A safety-alerting service used by field technicians failed overnight while two technicians were on a turbine. The incident was raised as P1, the on-call rota was activated, and the fault was traced to a third-party regional outage outside the application stack.

Being outside our stack did not make it outside our responsibility. Until the provider restored service the team maintained manual all-clear controls and sanitised the affected regions before closing the incident.

08 — What this demonstrates

Technology delivery tied directly to operational and commercial outcomes: a loss-making unit turned net-positive in six months by changing the operating model before the headcount, separating the commercial signals, charging for scope, and giving AI production authority only as fast as the evidence allowed.
