01 — Situation

A statutory commission still running on a spreadsheet

The 8th Central Pay Commission receives memoranda and demands from staff associations, ministries and federations, examines them, and places its recommendations. When I picked up the engagement that process was spreadsheet-driven, and the Commission itself operates against a fixed statutory tenure.

The platform had to serve 10 processing officers and roughly 100 ministry users, absorb a migration from 14 offices with no common data structure, and go live under coordination across four separate bodies.

02 — The constraint

The date was the one variable that could not absorb risk

On most programmes, when requirements churn or a dependency slips, the date moves. Here it could not: the Commission's tenure is set in statute. Every other variable had to take the strain instead — scope shape, sequencing, team structure, and how coordination was run.

  • A statutory tenure fixing the delivery date
  • A shared spreadsheet to be replaced by an accountable workflow
  • Volumes rising by an order of magnitude within months of launch
  • Four coordinating bodies with no direct reporting relationship
  • Migration from 14 offices with no shared structure
  • Government of India requirements for open-source technology and government-cloud hosting

03 — My mandate

Lead project delivery, product and workflow definition, and stakeholder coordination — then lead the transition from the initial operational platform toward an AI-assisted examination layer.

04 — Decisions

Decision 01

Parallelise the delivery rather than sequence it.

WhySequencing eight modules would have put every slip straight onto the statutory date. Structuring them as parallel streams across two sprints moved schedule risk into coordination, which I could actively manage, and out of the deadline, which I could not.

Trade-off acceptedParallel streams cost more coordination overhead and more of my own time in the middle of the programme. With seven people that was real. It bought a delivery shape where no single module could sink the date.

Decision 02

Turn the spreadsheet into a governed workflow, not a nicer spreadsheet.

WhyA shared file has no state, no owner and no audit trail, so work stalls invisibly. I specified the demand as a state machine — creation, examination, ministry reply, peer referral, reviewing officer, commission placement — with role-based access across the 10 processing officers and the ministry users.

Trade-off acceptedIt is slower to use than a spreadsheet for a single quick edit, and it forced officers to learn a process rather than a file. That is the cost of being able to say who holds a demand and what happened to it.

Decision 03

Build for month six, not launch day.

WhyThe system went live with roughly 1,500 memoranda and 50,000 demands. The known trajectory was 18,000 memoranda and some 200,000 demands as the next tranche of data arrives. Designing for launch-day volumes would have meant rebuilding inside the same statutory window.

Trade-off acceptedDesigning for ten times the live volume spent effort on load the platform did not yet carry, which is hard to justify against a four-week clock. It avoided a re-architecture during the Commission's tenure, when there would be no room for one.

05 — Delivery

Four bodies, no reporting line, one date

The only levers available across four bodies with no reporting relationship are institutional, not managerial. Coordination ran through the appropriate channels rather than through requests, and the 14-office migration was run as its own stream so it could not block module delivery.

When vendor resolution stalled, I escalated through the institutional channel and had the underlying SSL and routing fault diagnosed from the technology side in parallel. Against a statutory date, waiting on one track while the other was available was not a defensible choice.

Eight modules delivered in approximately four weeks with seven people, followed by live operation from August. The platform is designed for substantially larger demand volumes while remaining aligned to Government of India requirements for open-source technology and government-cloud hosting.

The AI layer, and what it is not allowed to do

I authored the AI scope now in build: anomaly detection, duplicate detection so the same demand arriving through several associations is recognised as one, evidence matching against the memoranda a demand cites, and retrieval of referenced sources. The objective is to stop officers reading every page to find the one that matters — not to automate the examination.

The division of labour is deliberate: Power BI provides analytical visualisation; AI provides the examination-support layer. Neither is asked to do the other's job, and neither makes the decision.

07 — What I'd carry forward

Where a date is genuinely immovable, the useful question is not how to go faster but which variable is allowed to absorb the risk. Naming that early — here, coordination overhead — is what makes the rest of the plan honest.

The other carry-forward is that escalation and diagnosis are parallel tracks, not a sequence. Raising an issue through the right channel does not mean waiting for it.

01 — Situation

There was no as-is process to digitise

The Green Hydrogen Certification of India scheme sits under the National Green Hydrogen Mission at MNRE. India did not have an established digital certification process for green hydrogen of this nature, which meant there was no mature process to take and automate.

So process definition, product design, technology delivery, stakeholder coordination, security certification and the future AI design all had to happen together rather than in sequence.

02 — The constraint

Define it, build it, and pass the audit — concurrently

  • No pre-existing certification process, and therefore no benchmark to build against
  • Four certificate types, each needing an auditable record of every amendment
  • A CERT-In empanelled security audit standing between the build and production
  • Validation rules that change through office memoranda, not through releases
  • Auditors who had never operated the digital process they were about to be measured on

03 — My mandate

Lead project delivery and UX, translate the emerging certification process into a production-ready national digital registry, and author the Phase 2 AI scope.

04 — Decisions

Decision 01

Design the registry append-only.

WhyA certificate is a legal artefact. If an amendment overwrites the original, the evidence of how the decision was reached is destroyed. An amendment therefore becomes a new record while the original stays intact, across all four certificate types.

Trade-off acceptedAppend-only costs storage, makes every read path more complex, and means the interface has to explain which version a user is looking at. It buys a trail that survives a challenge to a certification years later.

Decision 02

Run an internal audit before the external one.

WhyLetting a CERT-In empanelled audit be the moment issues surface puts the production date at the mercy of a report. The programme pre-audited internally and remediated in parallel, so the external audit met a platform that had already been attacked.

Trade-off acceptedIt pulled security effort forward into a window already competing with feature delivery, and some of that effort would have been unnecessary had the external audit been lenient. It produced a single-pass result instead of a remediation cycle.

Decision 03

Keep AI out of Phase 1 deliberately.

WhyAuditors needed to understand and operate the underlying process before they could meaningfully judge an assistant working on top of it. An AI suggestion nobody can evaluate is an AI suggestion nobody should trust.

Trade-off acceptedIt delayed the efficiency gain by a full phase, and meant launching with manual effort we knew could be reduced. It meant Phase 2 could be accepted on evidence rather than on faith.

05 — Delivery

Security as a delivery stream, not a gate at the end

48 of 51 findings were closed during internal pre-audit before the CERT-In empanelled audit opened, and the platform subsequently passed in a single pass.

The three remaining findings came from customised reusable components. They were manually verified and the residual risk was signed by the CTO rather than quietly carried — which is the difference between an accepted risk and an unowned one.

06 — Outcome

Junelive, with producers registering on launch day
48 / 51findings closed pre-audit
₹2 crPhase 2 AI scope authored
80–90%of that scope written by me

A national green hydrogen certification registry went live in June, with producers registering on the same public launch date.

The AI layer, and the two guardrails on it

I authored 80–90% of the Phase 2 AI scope — approximately ₹2 crore over about six months, spanning data scientists through testers: submission-gap analysis, validation against rules that change through office memoranda, auditor checklist pre-fill, emissions reconciliation, and evidence-backed assistance throughout.

AI advises. A named officer approves every consequential action.
Every generated claim cites the document supporting it.

Neither guardrail is negotiable, because an incorrect certification decision carries financial and legal consequences. Acceptance rests on a golden dataset: cases previously answered manually become the reference the AI is measured against. Because the process itself was new, the evaluation methodology had to be designed as part of the programme — there was no benchmark to borrow.

07 — What I'd carry forward

Sequencing is a product decision, and holding AI back a phase can be the thing that makes it adoptable. The question is not whether the model is good enough but whether the people accountable for the output can yet tell.

And where a process is genuinely new, the evaluation method is part of the deliverable. Designing acceptance criteria alongside the thing being accepted is slower, and it is the only way the result means anything.

01 — Situation

A unit at 100% loss, supporting hardware that does not stop

I inherited an application organisation supporting a wind portfolio at 100% loss, staffed at roughly twice the contractual requirement, under genuine round-the-clock operational obligations. Turbines do not stop at close of business, and neither can the applications watching them.

Scope covered roughly 50 to 55 applications and a team of about 25.

02 — The constraint

The obvious fix was the wrong one

The standard response to a loss-making unit staffed at twice requirement is to cut. That would have removed the cost and left the cause, because the cause was spread across six things at once:

  • Operating model
  • Knowledge management
  • SLA definition
  • Automation
  • Commercial scope
  • Team structure

Critical knowledge lived inside individual senior engineers. Support, enhancements and environment changes were measured as one blended pool, which hid where the service was actually failing. Out-of-scope work was absorbed rather than charged.

03 — My mandate

Own P&L, delivery, operational performance, product responsibilities and people management for the application organisation.

04 — Decisions

Decision 01

Fix the operating model before reducing people.

WhyCutting first removes capacity and keeps the dependency: the work still needs doing and only a few people can do it. I standardised SOPs so knowledge stopped living in individuals, shifted repetitive work left, and established an L1.5 layer — and only then reduced headcount.

Trade-off acceptedIt delayed the visible financial improvement by months while the cost base stayed where it was, which is an uncomfortable thing to hold in front of a P&L review. It made the reduction survivable rather than a transfer of risk onto the people who remained.

Decision 02

Separate the commercial signals.

WhySupport, enhancements and environment changes were being measured as one service pool, so failures in one averaged out against successes in another. Each got its own commitment, which made the actual failure visible and chargeable.

Trade-off acceptedThree commitments are harder to report and to govern than one blended number, and it removed a convenient place to hide. That was the point.

Decision 03

Commercialise scope instead of absorbing it.

WhyOut-of-scope requests were being delivered for free, which hid demand and funded a loss. They became separately scoped work, generating around $200,000 a year of new business.

Trade-off acceptedIt introduced friction into requests that previously just happened, and required holding a line with a client used to getting them. It converted a leak into revenue and made the real demand visible.

Decision 04

Give AI production authority only as fast as the evidence allowed.

WhyAI entered production support in controlled stages — shadow mode, then a six-hour window, then full-day operation — with each expansion resting on evidence from the stage before it. The environment combined rule-based scripting with agentic AI and AIOps on an internal platform.

Trade-off acceptedStaging it meant running human and machine in parallel for longer than strictly necessary, paying twice for the same coverage during shadow. It is why the expansion was never reversed.

05 — Delivery

When the safety alerting failed with technicians on a turbine

A safety-alerting service used by field technicians failed overnight while two technicians were on a turbine. I raised it as P1, activated the on-call rota, and the fault was traced to a third-party regional outage outside the application stack.

Outside our stack did not make it outside our responsibility. Until the provider restored service the team maintained manual all-clear controls and sanitised the affected regions before the incident was closed.

07 — What I'd carry forward

A unit losing money at twice the required headcount looks like a staffing problem and usually is not. Reduce people last: standardise the knowledge, move the work to the right tier, then resize. Reversing that order transfers the risk to whoever is left.

Design accountability into the workflow rather than digitising the paper one.

01 — Situation

An assessment of organisations that did not report to us

The National e-Governance Service Delivery Assessment measures how well government departments actually deliver digital services. I designed and operationalised the framework across more than 80 departments and ministries, spanning 150-plus parameters in 10–11 categories.

02 — The constraint

The hard part was adoption, not methodology

Participating organisations did not report to the programme team. An assessment nobody is obliged to take part in produces a partial dataset and a meaningless score, and no amount of rubric design fixes that.

There was a second problem underneath it: an automated score that domain auditors do not recognise will be rejected the first time it is inconvenient.

03 — My mandate

Design the benchmarking framework, validate its scoring, and make it operate across the participating organisations.

04 — Decisions

Decision 01

Use formal institutional authority rather than persuasion.

WhyWithout a reporting line, participation has to come from somewhere with standing. The programme ran through Secretary-level communication, CERT-In direction, and a named nodal officer in each organisation — then assessment, then remediation.

Trade-off acceptedIt is slower to start than an informal approach and spends institutional goodwill that cannot be spent twice. It produced participation that held across 80-plus organisations rather than the willing subset.

Decision 02

Validate the automated scoring against human auditors before trusting it.

WhyThe framework used AHP across 10–11 categories. I validated automated results against human auditors and tuned until they agreed to within ±10%, using sensitivity analysis to finalise the rubric.

Trade-off acceptedRunning both scoring methods in parallel roughly doubled the assessment effort for the first cycle. It is why the scores survived contact with the departments being scored.

02 — The constraint

Twenty-two languages, and a source ecosystem nothing off the shelf could ingest

Two constraints shaped the build. The sources were heterogeneous enough that an off-the-shelf ingestion model could not adequately handle them. And the platform had to work in 22 languages, where a plausible-sounding mistranslation of a government service is worse than no translation at all.

03 — My mandate

Work from the PMO side on platform strategy, product definition, and the language quality system.

04 — Decisions

Decision 01

Treat translation as a measurable quality problem, not a model choice.

WhyRather than selecting a translation engine and accepting its output, the programme instrumented quality: machine translation, two reviewers per language at launch, corrections fed back into the model, language-specific terminology, and native-speaker intervention where it was needed.

Trade-off acceptedTwo reviewers per language across 22 languages is a standing cost, and it slowed launch. It produced a number we could manage against — an initial error rate near one sentence in ten, with roughly 80% of output accepted as translated.

Decision 02

Graduate review by traffic and measured quality.

WhyReviewing every language equally forever would have been unaffordable and wrong: a high-traffic language with a poor error rate needs more attention than a low-traffic one performing well. Review intensity was graduated on both signals.

Trade-off acceptedIt accepted that lower-traffic languages would get less scrutiny, which is a real quality trade-off and had to be stated rather than hidden. It made the review model sustainable past launch.

Decision 03

Build a custom CMS rather than adopt an ingestion product.

WhyThe heterogeneous source ecosystem could not be adequately handled by an off-the-shelf ingestion model, so the programme specified a custom CMS and then semantic search, recommendations and summarisation on top of it.

Trade-off acceptedCustom means owning maintenance and upgrades that a product vendor would otherwise carry. It was the only way to federate sources that did not agree on structure.

05 — Delivery

The language pipeline

  • Machine translation
  • Two reviewers per language
  • Correction feedback
  • Terminology
  • Graduated review

Corrections were not just fixes: they were training signal and terminology, which is why the error rate was something the programme could move rather than simply report.

06 — Outcome

A single front door over twenty-plus federated sources, with a language quality system that could be measured and improved, and semantic search, recommendations and summarisation specified on a custom CMS for the following phase.

07 — What I'd carry forward

AI quality is an operating process, not a model selection. The reviewers, the feedback loop, the terminology and the graduation rule were the product; the model was a component.

Instrumenting quality before scaling is what made the trade-offs explicit. Without a measured error rate there is no honest way to decide which languages get less review.

01 — Situation

Credential verification, and the friction around it

Skillchain took a blockchain credential platform from problem statement to a unit-wide EY pilot. Credentials were issued as NFTs on Quorum, a private Ethereum implementation. I held the architecture and acted as de facto product owner.

02 — The constraint

The hard problem was adoption, not cryptography

The cryptography was well understood and the platform was buildable. What was not guaranteed was that anyone would use it: participation was voluntary, and the value of a credential network is a function of how many people are on it.

The stakeholders were also not one group. HR, talent acquisition, hiring managers and external verification vendors each wanted something different from the same record.

03 — My mandate

Architect the platform and own the product decisions — requirements, scope, and what made the first release.

04 — Decisions

Decision 01

Develop requirements across all four stakeholder groups before building.

WhyA credential platform that serves HR but not hiring managers gets issued and never read. I developed requirements across HR, talent acquisition, hiring managers and verification vendors, and took the product through three rounds of partner and director review before development started.

Trade-off acceptedThree review rounds before a line of code is a long time to hold a team, and it delayed the first demonstrable thing. It meant the pilot launched into demand rather than into indifference.

Decision 02

Descope the NFT marketplace to protect the core.

WhyThe marketplace was the most interesting part of the concept and the least essential to the credential's actual job. Carrying it would have put the core verification capability at risk of arriving late or half-built. I cut it.

Trade-off acceptedIt removed the feature the concept was most often sold on, and with it a plausible route to wider network effects. The core shipped and reached roughly 80% voluntary uptake.

A pilot people opted into

Where adoption is voluntary, requirements have to come from everyone who touches the record. A credential nobody reads is not a credential.

01 — Situation

A manual process on a legacy platform

A UK BFSI client reconciled Visa chargebacks manually on a legacy Unisys Mapper environment. The cost showed up in four places: reconciliation effort, priority-one callouts, contractual penalties, and refund leakage.

Over this period I progressed from mainframe support and development into owning the application outright from offshore, then into blockchain architecture.

02 — The constraint

The permission had never existed

The automation itself was tractable. What blocked it was that the required Mapper permissions had never been configured in the environment — not revoked, not misplaced, never created. That is not a defect anyone can fix in code.

This was also a production financial process. A reconciliation that silently mis-states a chargeback creates a refund problem, not just a report problem.

03 — My mandate

Automate the reconciliation, and own the application that resulted.

04 — Decisions

Decision 01

Resolve the permission commercially rather than engineer around it.

WhyThere was a technical workaround available, and it would have left a fragile dependency in a financial process permanently. I took the constraint to the vendor and the client as a commercial and operational issue and had the permission created.

Trade-off acceptedIt put the delivery date in someone else's hands and cost weeks of negotiation that looked like no progress. It produced an automation that rests on a supported configuration rather than a workaround nobody would remember in two years.

Decision 02

Rebuild the pipeline rather than script over the manual steps.

WhyThe implementation rebuilt the pipeline across COBOL file handling and triggered-job state persistence, so the process had state it could recover from rather than a sequence of steps that had to be repeated on failure.

Trade-off acceptedMore effort than scripting the happy path, and more to understand for whoever inherited it. Reported defects fell 40%, measured six months after go-live.

05 — Delivery

Measured by the client, not by me

The client calculated approximately £200,000 in annual savings across reconciliation effort, priority-one callouts, contractual penalties and refund leakage. The figure is theirs, over a full year, not a projection of mine.

Alongside this I developed blockchain proofs of concept that reached production for external clients — Hyperledger sample tracking for Boston Scientific, document management for Sanofi — and established the first blockchain lab inside TCS infrastructure.

06 — Outcome

~£200,000annual saving, client-calculated
40%fewer reported defects at six months
2blockchain POCs into production
1stblockchain lab inside TCS infrastructure

07 — What I'd carry forward

The constraint preventing delivery is often not the problem visible in the ticket. This is where I learned it, and it has shaped how I run discovery since: the CRCS statute and the GHCI audit sequence are the same lesson at a larger scale.

A workaround that ships on time can be more expensive than a negotiation that does not. In a financial process, it usually is.
