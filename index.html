Also delivered

Five further engagements, in brief.

NeSDA Benchmarking Framework

NeGD / MeitY · Senior Consultant · 2022–24

Designed and operationalised a benchmarking framework across 80-plus departments and ministries using AHP across 10–11 categories. The challenge was institutional adoption: participating organisations did not report to the programme team, so the programme used formal authority — Secretary-level communication, CERT-In direction, a named nodal officer — rather than persuasion.

Automated results were validated against human auditors to within ±10%, with sensitivity analysis used to finalise the rubric. Results reached the Cabinet Secretary's monthly meeting and the Samiksha portal under PMO monitoring; G20 benchmarking placed India in the top five. The framework now operates as AKAM — nothing goes onto NIC infrastructure without passing it.

Demonstrates · Digital governance · Evaluation systems · Institutional change · Automated assessment · Executive reporting

IVFRT 2.0

MHA — Bureau of Immigration · Senior Consultant · 2022–24

Consolidation of visa, e-visa, OCI and NRI services onto one platform through staged national rollout. The transformation challenge was operational fragility across separate databases and application silos, combined with long-established local processes across ports.

I led UI/UX and process re-engineering with business analysts, developers and designers, working from observation of officers and travellers rather than written specifications alone. Collapsing the siloed databases into one analysable store was deliberately deferred to protect port and core-system stability. Deployment began with a two-seaport pilot; the platform is live across seaports today, with airports piloting and land ports to follow.

Demonstrates · National-scale transformation · UX leadership · Process standardisation · Operational resilience · Technology sequencing

National Portal of India v2.0

Government of India · Senior Consultant, PMO · 2022–24

Turning a static directory of disconnected government portals into a single digital front door, federating 20-plus sources including One District One Product, iGOT, e-Courts, MyScheme, the services portal, Aadhaar, income-tax verification, tourism, government directories and live news.

The 22-language translation problem was treated as a measurable quality problem: machine translation, two reviewers per language at launch, correction feedback into the model, language-specific terminology, native-speaker intervention, and review graduated by traffic and quality. The initial error rate was near one sentence in ten, with roughly 80% of output accepted as translated. The programme then specified semantic search, recommendations and summarisation on a custom CMS, because the heterogeneous source ecosystem could not be handled by an off-the-shelf ingestion model.

Demonstrates · AI quality systems · Human-in-the-loop design · Multilingual technology · Platform integration · Product strategy

Skillchain

UST Global, for EYGDS · Blockchain Architect, de facto Product Owner · 2021–22

Took a blockchain credential platform from problem statement to unit-wide EY pilot with roughly 80% voluntary uptake. The primary challenge proved to be adoption rather than cryptography: requirements were developed across HR, talent acquisition, hiring managers and verification vendors, and the product went through three rounds of partner and director review before development began.

Built on Quorum, a private Ethereum implementation, with credentials issued as NFTs. The NFT marketplace was deliberately descoped to protect the core delivery. The underlying verification capability continued through 2024 and now exists in EY Skills and EY Badges on Degreed.

Demonstrates · Product ownership · Blockchain architecture · Adoption strategy · Scope management

Mainframe Automation & Blockchain POCs

TCS — UK BFSI client · IT Analyst · 2015–20

Automated a manual Visa chargeback reconciliation process on a legacy Unisys Mapper environment, rebuilding the pipeline across COBOL file handling and triggered-job state persistence. The client calculated approximately £200,000 in annual savings across reconciliation effort, priority-one callouts, contractual penalties and refund leakage. Reported defects fell 40%, measured six months after go-live.

The major blocker was not code: the required Mapper permissions had never existed in the environment, and resolving that took vendor and client negotiation rather than engineering. Blockchain proofs of concept also reached production for external clients — Hyperledger sample tracking for Boston Scientific, document management for Sanofi — and I established the first blockchain lab inside TCS infrastructure.

05Applied AI

How I think about applied AI

Six positions that have survived contact with regulated government workflows and 24×7 enterprise operations.

01 — Workflow

Start with the workflow, not the model

The first question is not which model to use. It is: what decision, task, or bottleneck are we trying to improve? AI is valuable when it changes the economics, speed, quality, or accessibility of a real workflow.

02 — Boundary

Define the AI boundary

For regulated or consequential workflows, AI should not automatically inherit authority. I define:

  • What AI can recommend
  • What AI can automate
  • What requires human approval
  • What evidence must accompany an output
  • What happens when confidence is insufficient

03 — Evaluation

Build evaluation before declaring success

Acceptance cannot rest on a model's generic benchmark. Where possible I use golden datasets, previously human-reviewed cases, human-versus-machine comparison, accuracy and exception analysis, sensitivity analysis, domain-expert review, and operational metrics.

04 — Autonomy

Introduce autonomy progressively

A pattern used in enterprise production support, where each expansion of authority rested on evidence from the stage before it:

  • Shadow
  • Limited authority
  • Expanded window
  • Production

05 — Provenance

Make outputs traceable

For government and regulated systems, an AI answer without provenance is often not enough. Generated outputs are tied back to the document that supports them, and a named human remains accountable for the decision.

  • Data
  • AI / model
  • Evidence
  • Validation
  • Human decision
  • Auditable outcome

06 — Measurement

Measure the system, not just the model

The success metric may be processing time, MTTR, SLA, false positives, human acceptance, cost, throughput, adoption, auditability, or business outcome. The model is only one component of the product.

AI advises. A named officer decides. The first of two non-negotiable guardrails on the GHCI certification registry. The second: every generated claim cites the document supporting it. An incorrect certification decision carries financial and legal consequences.

10How I operate

Four working principles

01

Start with the constraint

Understand what cannot move — law, policy, security, procurement, timeline, budget, organisational structure — before deciding what to build. On the CRCS portal the immovable constraint was a statute, not a system; the 8th CPC deadline was statutory, so schedule risk had to be absorbed in the delivery shape rather than negotiated away.

02

Make the decision explicit

Important decisions should not sit buried inside project documentation. State what was decided, why, and which trade-off was accepted. Deferring the collapse of IVFRT's siloed databases cost analytical convenience and bought port stability — that is a decision, and it should be legible as one.

03

Make ownership visible

Every consequential workflow needs an owner, a decision authority, an escalation path, and accountability. That is why CRCS escalates automatically at five, seven and fifteen days, why the 8th CPC lifecycle is role-based across ten processing officers and some hundred ministry users, and why GHCI's AI advises while a named officer approves.

04

Measure the outcome

"Delivered successfully" is not a result. What changed? Processing time, availability, profitability, scale, adoption, defect rate, operational workload. Every number on this site is one of those answers.

My career began in engineering and blockchain architecture and evolved into programme delivery, product ownership, technology consulting, and transformation leadership.

Today I work at the intersection of applied AI, digital public infrastructure, enterprise technology, and programme execution — turning complex requirements into products that can operate at scale and stand up to regulatory, security, and operational scrutiny. AI is the next step along that line rather than a change of direction: the same question I have been answering since the mainframe work, which is what the system is actually allowed to decide, and who answers for it.

My programmes have included national platforms serving tens of millions of users, regulated digital registries, national benchmarking frameworks, immigration platforms, renewable-energy operations, and AI-assisted government workflows.

Technology transformation succeeds when the product, operating model, governance, and technology architecture are designed together. My career has increasingly moved toward problems where the answer cannot be found in technology alone. A statute may need to change. A procurement model may need to change. An organisation may need to adopt a new workflow. A vendor may need to be held to a specification. An AI system may need a human accountability boundary. An architecture may need to prioritise resilience over analytical elegance. A programme may have four weeks rather than four months. Those are the environments where I add the most value.
