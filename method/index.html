Method

How I run a programme

AI-DLC is my name for an AI-assisted software lifecycle: AI support at every stage, from discovery through requirements, review and delivery to documentation. It is not the same as building AI features into a workflow. That is a separate question, covered under Applied AI.

The lifecycle

Six stages. AI helps at each one, and a person signs each one.

The examples come from the GHCI delivery. The artefacts and sign-offs are the ones I run on government programmes.

01

Discover

Artefact
Problem statement, stakeholder map
Signed by
Client sponsor
Where AI helps
Requirement and impact analysis, workflow simplification, meeting notes turned into minutes and Records of Discussion.
What a person checks
Every AI output is reviewed before use. No real customer data or credentials go into a prompt.
02

BRD

Artefact
Business requirements document
Signed by
Client head
Where AI helps
Drafting documents and workflows. A swimlane diagram of the certification flow was shared with the ministry.
What a person checks
Business rules checked against the scheme. Formal client sign-off before any sprint starts.
03

SRS

Artefact
Software requirements specification
Signed by
Client and tech leads
Where AI helps
Module planning, architecture validation, schema design and risk review by the Tech Lead. Sequence diagrams for design review.
What a person checks
Tech Lead and BA review the schema and the requirement coverage.
04

Sprints

Artefact
Backlog, sprint plan, demos
Signed by
Product owner
Where AI helps
Sprint plan drafts. HTML mock-ups as a design reference. Screen-to-code, bug fixing and API integration for developers.
What a person checks
The developer owns 100% of merged code. The designer reviews UI mismatches. Most tasks needed one or two refinement prompts.
05

Build and accept

Artefact
UAT report, security report
Signed by
Nodal officer
Where AI helps
Test cases from Figma pages, test plans, UAT scripts, bug reports, and a code-review pass for security, performance and accessibility.
What a person checks
QA runs the tests; generic prompts scored 2 out of 5. Role owners sign the UAT release note. An external audit, with fixes and revalidation, precedes the certificate.
06

Release and measure

Artefact
Go-live note, results review
Signed by
Sponsor
Where AI helps
Held out of Phase 1 by design. In the product itself, AI arrives in Phase 2, behind the guardrails.
What a person checks
The sponsor reviews results against the baseline.
AIproposes, drafts, checks and summarises
Personreviews, signs and answers for the result

What went wrong

Generic prompts scored 2 out of 5 in QA and missed high-severity cases. Generated UI needed a designer's review, because spacing, fonts and placement were often off. Nearly every task took one or two refinement prompts.

The guardrails

No personal data or credentials in a prompt. Every output reviewed by a person. The developer owns all merged code. No hard-coded secrets.

Traceability

One requirement, followed to the bug list

The SRS carried close to 250 requirements. A requirements traceability matrix mapped each one to a function and then to the feature built, and the feature list and bug list came from it.

  1. Requirementmodule-prefixed ID
  2. Function
  3. Feature
  4. Bug listand tests

IDs carry a module prefix rather than one running number, for example AUTH, APP, WF, PAY, CERT, ACV and NFR. Tracking ran in OpenForge, an internal NIC tool, with Figma for design and shared trackers for bugs and AI usage.

Governance rhythm

What is fixed upfront, and what stays open

Scope
The BRD is signed by the client before sprint one. After sign-off nothing in it changes. New requests become change requests, recorded in an annexure to the BRD.
Sprints
Two weeks. A demo every three sprints.
Risks
A risk register that I own, reviewed fortnightly, with an owner and a mitigation against each client-side blocker.
Records
Records of Discussion with the client after each substantive meeting, and a Director-level review with status and open items by owner.
Working principles

Four I keep coming back to

These are drawn from the case studies.

01

Start with what cannot move

Law, policy, security, procurement, the calendar, the budget, the organisation chart. On CRCS the immovable thing was a statute. On the 8th CPC it was a date written into legislation.

02

State the decision and its cost

A decision with no stated trade-off is a preference. Deferring the single data store on IVFRT cost analytical capability and bought port stability.

03

Give AI authority only as fast as evidence allows

Shadow, then a limited window, then production. Advisory output, a named person approving anything consequential, every claim citing its source.

04

Measure the outcome, not the deliverable

"Delivered successfully" is not a result. Processing time, availability, profit, adoption, defect rate, workload.

Government delivery

What I adapt that a private-sector programme manager might not

Approvals

Gates outside the team's control

An approval from the client or a senior authority can decide the plan. Phase 2 of GHCI needed client approval before it could be procured. I plan around the gate and track it as a risk.

Procurement

Who is allowed to bid

Government procurement narrows the field and sets the evaluation. Scope and acceptance criteria have to be written so a procurement process can run on them.

Security and data rules

Audit before launch, data in India

External security testing to CERT-In guidelines before go-live, data protection law and Indian data residency shape the architecture from the first sprint.

Other systems

Dependencies I do not control

Sign-in, eSign, payment and language services run by other departments. Each is a dependency with an owner, a date and a fallback.
