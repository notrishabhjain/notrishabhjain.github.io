## Context

I took the transition of a large application portfolio for GE Vernova: a heavily
over-staffed team, an operation running at a loss to the account, a strained
relationship with the client's CIO and application owners, and incidents taking four
days to resolve that should have closed in hours.

## The problem

Three problems wearing each other's clothes.

The **commercial** problem was that the account was a cash outflow — the operation cost
more to run than it earned, and no amount of goodwill was going to fix that.

The **structural** problem was that the team was roughly twice the size the work
required. Over-staffing looks like safety and behaves like the opposite: with no clear
ownership, people duplicate each other, collide on the same incidents, and work weekends
on things that were never theirs. An inflated team with an unclear RACI is not a team
with slack. It's a team with confusion, and confusion is expensive in both money and
people.

The **technical** problem was real too — manual deployments, scattered repositories, no
reliable path from a reported incident to a fix.

## What I owned

- **Right-sizing.** I brought the team down to the size the work actually needed —
  roughly halving it — and defined RACI matrices across vendors and the client so that
  what remained had unambiguous ownership rather than shared guesswork.
- **Automation and standardisation.** Day-to-day operations, reporting, budgeting, and
  client communication moved from ad-hoc to standard. Repository migrations and
  deployment script automation replaced the manual paths.
- **Governance.** SOPs, SOWs, delivery governance, and KPI-led tracking across a $2M+
  annual delivery portfolio, with an escalation framework so an incident had a route
  instead of an argument.
- **The client relationship.** Structured reporting on a fixed cadence, so the CIO
  stopped learning about problems from their own users.
- **The record.** As-Is and To-Be workflow documentation per application team, cutting
  handoff ambiguity and the overhead that came with it.

## Outcome

- The account moved from **cash outflow to net positive** across the entire engagement.
- **~$500K in additional business** won over the following year.
- **Mean time to resolution: four days to under twelve hours.**
- **~50% efficiency improvement**, with the team right-sized by roughly half.
- CIO and application owner relationships recovered to the point where the conversation
  was about roadmap rather than escalation.

## What I'd point to

This is the case study I'd lead with for a portfolio role, because it's the one that
ends in a P&L number rather than a delivery metric. Anyone can make a team faster by
adding people. Making an account profitable while making it faster, with half the team,
is a different job.

I'd also be honest about what that job involves. Right-sizing a team means people leave,
and I'd rather say that plainly than dress it up as optimisation. What I'd defend is the
alternative: an account bleeding money does not survive to employ anyone, and a team
where nobody knows who owns an incident burns its people out long before the budget runs
out.
