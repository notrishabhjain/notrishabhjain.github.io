## Context

I inherited a large application portfolio in trouble: a 30+ person team across multiple
vendors, a strained relationship with the client's CIO and application owners, and a
support function that took days to resolve incidents it should have closed in hours.

## The problem

Two problems that looked like one. The technical problem was real — manual deployments,
scattered repositories, no reliable path from a reported incident to a fix. But the
reason it persisted was that nobody could say who owned what. In a multi-vendor estate,
ambiguity isn't a documentation gap; it's a delay multiplier, and it burns the people
caught in it.

## What I owned

- **Ownership, first.** RACI matrices across the vendors and the client, plus an
  escalation governance framework, so an incident had a path instead of an argument.
- **The technical transition.** Repository migrations and deployment script automation —
  the unglamorous work that turns a four-day resolution into a twelve-hour one.
- **The record.** As-Is and To-Be workflow documentation for each application team,
  which cut handoff ambiguity and the operational overhead that came with it.
- **The relationship.** Structured reporting on a fixed cadence, so the CIO stopped
  finding out about problems from their own users.
- **The portfolio.** SOPs, SOWs, delivery governance, and KPI-led tracking across a
  $2M+ annual delivery portfolio in the business unit I handled.

## Outcome

Mean time to resolution fell from four days to under twelve hours. Burnout and attrition
on the team dropped materially. The CIO and application owner relationships recovered to
the point where the conversation was about roadmap rather than escalation.

## What I'd point to

The attrition number, if we can get one. `[VERIFY]` — do you have a before/after on team
attrition or team size retained? For an employer audience this is the single most

I'd also say plainly: I treat burnout as a delivery risk, not an HR issue. A team that
can't tell you who owns an incident is a team that will lose people, and losing people
is what makes the next incident worse.
