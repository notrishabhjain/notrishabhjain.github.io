## Context

UST wanted verifiable internal credentials — a way for an employee's skills and
certifications to be trusted without a person in the middle vouching for them. The
technology was the easy part.

## The problem

Blockchain credentialing demos are trivial to build and almost never get used. The
hard question isn't whether you can write an immutable credential; it's whether 2,500
people can be bothered to, and whether anyone downstream trusts it enough to act on it.
A credential nobody checks is a database with extra steps.

## What I owned

I defined the product vision and roadmap, and — more usefully — ran the user research
and feedback cycles that turned a distributed ledger capability into behaviour people
would actually adopt. That meant repeatedly cutting things that were technically
interesting and didn't change whether someone used the platform on a Tuesday.

## Outcome

Secure credential transactions across 2,500+ employees, with adoption driven by the
research cycles rather than by mandate.

## What I'd point to

This is the clearest product management case study I have, and it's here for that
reason. It's also where I learned the thing I've applied on every government platform
since: a technically correct system that people route around has failed, and it has
failed for product reasons, not engineering ones.
